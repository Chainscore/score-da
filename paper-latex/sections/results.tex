\subsection{Observation Window and Protocol Eras}
\label{sec:results-eras}

Our window (November~15, 2025 to February~14, 2026) contains several parameter changes, which allow before--after comparisons:
\squishlist
    \item \textbf{Ethereum}: four regimes: Pectra (target~6/max~9 blobs), Fusaka (same blob parameters, plus PeerDAS and the EIP-7918 reserve price, from Dec~3), BPO1 (target~10/max~15, from Dec~9), and BPO2 (target~14/max~21, from Jan~7).
    \item \textbf{Celestia}: app version~6 from Nov~24, and a maximum-square increase from 128 to 256 (8 to 32~MiB per block) on Dec~3.
    \item \textbf{Polkadot, Espresso, NEAR, Avail}: no major parameter changes.
\squishend
Table~\ref{tab:feature_comparison} lists the active parameters.

Ethereum has the highest utilization: $\sim$60\% of its maximum under Pectra and Fusaka ($\sim$90\% of the target), falling to $\sim$31\% after BPO1 and $\sim$23\% after BPO2 as capacity grew faster than demand.
The other five protocols stay below 3\% on average: Polkadot's estimated data utilization is $\sim$3\% (per relay block, $\sim$26 of 100 cores carry an included candidate; Section~\ref{sec:results-blocks}), Celestia's is $\le$1\% outside the December load test, and Espresso, Avail and NEAR are at or below 1\%.
Observed throughput therefore reflects demand; capacity is given by the protocol maximum (Table~\ref{tab:throughput_summary}).
Volumes over the window were 9--37~TiB for Polkadot (estimated), $\sim$787~GiB for NEAR, $\sim$614~GiB for Celestia and $\sim$392~GiB for Ethereum. These figures are not like for like: NEAR's is total chunk data (transactions and receipts) of a general-purpose chain, not rollup DA payload, and Polkadot's includes parachain state witnesses.

Median DA cost spans nearly seven orders of magnitude, from \$3.1$\times$10$^{-9}$/MiB on Espresso to \$0.020/MiB on Celestia, reflecting differences in pricing mechanism.
Espresso's fee has been fixed at 1~wei/byte since genesis, so its cost follows ETH/USD, falling from $\sim$\$3.5$\times$10$^{-9}$ to $\sim$\$2$\times$10$^{-9}$ as ETH fell; in the permissioned Mainnet~0 it is a placeholder and does not reflect demand.

Before Fusaka, Ethereum's blob base fee sat at the 1-wei floor on $\sim$28\% of blocks and the median cost was $\sim$\$4$\times$10$^{-9}$/MiB.
From the first day after Fusaka no block is at the floor and the median rises six orders of magnitude to $\sim$\$0.009/MiB.
BPO1 had not yet added capacity, so the change is consistent with EIP-7918, the only fee-relevant change at that boundary (Section~\ref{sec:results-upgrades}).
Ethereum's VWAP (\$0.18/MiB) is far above its median because two days, November~18 and December~4, account for 78\% of window blob spend; excluding them, VWAP is \$0.040/MiB. The December~4 spike (daily p90 \$0.56/MiB) is a transient overshoot immediately after Fusaka activation.

Avail's median (\$0.0096/MiB) is close to Celestia's, but its daily median exceeds \$1/MiB on December~1--3, days of low volume (45--50~MiB/day) and many small submissions; its p90 stays near \$1.1/MiB through late December. This is consistent with per-transaction fees dominating the per-MiB cost of small submissions; we do not isolate the contribution of Avail's fee modifier.
NEAR has no blob type or DA fee market, so we price DA as the marginal gas of posting 1~MiB as function-call input (Section~\ref{sec:meth-metrics}): $\sim$0.006~NEAR, or $\sim$\$0.010/MiB at the window's prices. The gas price stayed at its 10$^{8}$ yoctoNEAR minimum throughout, so the USD cost moves only with the NEAR price. NEAR's chain-wide average fee per byte of chunk data is far higher ($\sim$\$0.53/MiB) because most of its gas pays for contract execution, not data.
Polkadot's bulk coretime price, observed on 14 days, is below \$10$^{-4}$/MiB on a capacity basis; it covers execution as well as DA.

Short retention multiplies cost: NEAR's 5-epoch garbage-collection window ($\sim$1.5~days) requires $\sim$237 reposts per year and gives the highest annualized cost ($\sim$\$2.4/MiB/yr, about twice Celestia's), while Celestia's 7-day sampling window lifts its \$0.02/MiB spot cost to $\sim$\$1/MiB/yr.

\subsection{Protocol Upgrades}
\label{sec:results-upgrades}

\subsubsection{Ethereum: Pectra $\rightarrow$ Fusaka $\rightarrow$ BPO1 $\rightarrow$ BPO2}

Fig.~\ref{fig:eth} separates the effects of the three upgrades.
Under Pectra the blob fee was at the 1-wei minimum on 5--68\% of blocks per day.
Fusaka's EIP-7918 ties a reserve price to the execution base fee; from December~4 no block is at the floor (Fig.~\ref{fig:eth}(c)) and the median cost rises to $\sim$\$0.009/MiB, while utilization stays at $\sim$58\%.
BPO1 and BPO2 each lowered utilization (from $\sim$58\% to $\sim$31\% to $\sim$23\%) and target utilization (from $\sim$87\% to $\sim$47\% to $\sim$34\%; Fig.~\ref{fig:eth}(b)).
The median cost stayed between \$0.007 and \$0.010/MiB in both BPO eras (Fig.~\ref{fig:eth}(a)), consistent with fees held near the EIP-7918 reserve price rather than set by congestion; we did not collect the execution base fee, so this is not verified against the reserve price directly.
Daily blob volume stayed at $\sim$4.1--4.7~GiB in all four eras, so the added capacity was not used during the window.

\subsubsection{Celestia: December Load Test}

Celestia raised its maximum square from 128 to 256 on December~3, quadrupling capacity to $\sim$5.3~MiB/s.
From December~16 to~21, daily throughput rose eighty-fold, from a baseline of $\sim$0.015~MiB/s to $\sim$1.2~MiB/s ($\sim$7.3~MiB per block). Celestia's status page announced on December~16 that a partner would run mainnet tests adding about 1~MB/s; the load began a few hours before the notice. It reached $\sim$22\% of the new ceiling, and would have used $\sim$90\% of the pre-December capacity of 1.33~MiB/s.
Cost did not rise: the daily median fell slightly (\$0.020 to \$0.017/MiB) and the daily p90 fell from $\sim$\$0.046 to $\sim$\$0.018/MiB, close to the median, as the test posted large blobs at the minimum gas price. Afterwards throughput and the p90 returned to their earlier levels; the largest load in the window did not produce congestion.

\subsection{Block Dynamics and Polkadot Core Occupancy}
\label{sec:results-blocks}

Fig.~\ref{fig:blocks}(a--b) shows block time distributions for the two protocols where they are informative; the fixed-cadence protocols (Ethereum 12\,s, Polkadot 6\,s, Avail 20\,s) are omitted.
Celestia holds 5--6\,s after an elevated p90 in mid-November.
Espresso's responsive HotShot consensus has p10~$\sim$1\,s and p50~$\sim$2\,s, with a p90 that falls from $\sim$6.5\,s in the first weeks to $\sim$3\,s by February.

Fig.~\ref{fig:blocks}(c) shows Polkadot's per-block core occupancy: $\sim$26 of 100 cores carry an included candidate in a typical relay block. Per-block occupancy understates the number of cores in use: on-chain core descriptors assign $\sim$58--62 cores to bulk coretime (50--55 parachains) and the remaining $\sim$40 to the on-demand pool, and on the sampled days (November~18, January~6, February~10) 41 distinct cores carried at least one candidate. Many parachains produce a block every 12\,s and so occupy their core in alternate relay blocks; the unused capacity is mainly the on-demand pool and $\sim$20 bulk-assigned cores with no inclusions.
