\subsection{Trade-offs Between the Three Paradigms}
\label{sec:disc-paradigms}

\paragraph{Sampling protocols (Celestia, Avail, Ethereum)}
Celestia and Avail give light clients the strongest guarantees among the six designs, because a resource-constrained node can check availability itself; Ethereum's PeerDAS samples at full nodes only.
These guarantees weaken if too few nodes sample, a dependence the other two paradigms do not have.

\paragraph{Validator-recovery protocols (Polkadot, NEAR)}
Among the six designs, Polkadot's inclusion rule is the most direct enforcement: a candidate is included only after $\geq 2/3$ of availability bitfields attest to chunk custody, and finality is then gated on approval checking.
In exchange, light clients cannot verify availability themselves.
For parachain validation this is acceptable, because the relay chain validators are the main consumers of the data; an external rollup using Polkadot or NEAR for DA would have to trust the validator set.

\paragraph{VID protocol (Espresso)}
VID disperses shares across the whole operator set, so making certified data unrecoverable requires corrupting a large fraction of operators rather than a small committee. In Mainnet~0 the practical value of this property is limited while the operator set is permissioned (20~operators) and there is no slashing.
The 1~wei/byte fee is likewise a placeholder.
Whether VID's advantages matter in practice will depend on a permissionless deployment with economic security behind it.

\subsection{The Cost--Throughput Frontier}
\label{sec:disc-frontier}

Fig.~\ref{fig:landscape}(a) plots median cost against protocol maximum throughput; across the six protocols, cost and maximum throughput are not inversely related.
Espresso combines low cost with moderate throughput, but its cost is a Mainnet~0 placeholder.
Celestia and Avail have moderate throughput at moderate cost.
Polkadot has the highest maximum throughput ($\sim$167~MiB/s) at a capacity price of $\sim$\$5$\times$10$^{-6}$/MiB, which also covers execution.
NEAR combines high capacity ($\sim$59~MiB/s) with a marginal DA cost of $\sim$\$0.010/MiB, close to Avail and Ethereum, but its short retention makes it the most expensive layer once cost is annualized.
After Fusaka, Ethereum offers modest capacity at a cost comparable to Celestia and Avail, with the largest validator set and stake behind it.

The protocols were built for different jobs: Polkadot for parachain validation, NEAR for general smart-contract execution, Ethereum for censorship resistance and economic security, and Celestia and Avail as dedicated DA layers.
Cost and throughput therefore do not rank them on their own; retention, trust assumptions, light-client verification and ecosystem integration matter as much.

\subsection{Polkadot's Scaling Headroom}
\label{sec:disc-polkadot}
About 41 of Polkadot's 100 cores carried candidates on the sampled days, and the on-demand pool ($\sim$40 cores) had no inclusions on November~18, so the relay chain can absorb more parachain throughput without parameter changes. Estimated DA throughput (1.2--4.9~MiB/s, from parachain-side PoV sizes) is 1--3\% of the $\sim$167~MiB/s maximum.
Bulk coretime prices would be expected to rise with demand; at the observed capacity price, cost is not a binding constraint.
A more precise analysis would need compressed PoV sizes, which only active validators record.\footnote{The \texttt{candidate\_validation\_pov\_size} histogram exported by Polkadot validators.}

\subsection{Implications for Rollup Developers}
\label{sec:disc-rollups}

For optimistic rollups, the retention horizon constrains the choice of DA layer independently of cost.
An optimistic rollup with a 7-day challenge period cannot rely on Polkadot (25~h) or NEAR ($\sim$1.5~d) without its own archive, and Celestia's 7-day sampling window leaves no margin.
For the same reason, spot prices understate the cost of sustained availability on short-retention layers; the annualized cost (Section~\ref{sec:meth-metrics}) counts the reposts needed to keep data available.
In the window, minimum fees and token prices, more than congestion, set what rollups paid: Ethereum's post-Fusaka cost is consistent with the EIP-7918 reserve price, and Celestia's six-day partner load test at $\sim$22\% of capacity did not raise its median fee.
Only Ethereum used a large share of its capacity, and only before the BPO forks; fee-market behaviour under sustained load remains to be observed.

\subsection{Protocol Design Observations}
\label{sec:disc-design}

\paragraph{Withholding enforcement}
Slashing for omission faults requires attributable evidence, which none of the six designs produces; commission faults such as equivocation or invalid execution are attributable.
Other enforcement models may be needed, for example insurance schemes, reputation systems with gradual stake reduction, or cryptographic mechanisms that make withholding unprofitable without having to attribute it.

\paragraph{Retention}
Four protocols have a default retention horizon, and Avail and Espresso retain data unless operators enable pruning.
Bounded retention is a deliberate choice that limits node storage growth; a DA layer is meant to provide a window for verification and fraud detection.
Rollups and applications that need data beyond that window must store it elsewhere, for example on IPFS, Filecoin or Arweave.

\subsection{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:disc-limitations}

\paragraph{Demand-constrained measurements}
Observed throughput reflects demand during one 92-day window; conclusions about maximum throughput must come from protocol parameters (Section~\ref{sec:meth-normalization}).

\paragraph{Token prices}
USD costs depend on token prices, which moved widely during the window (daily means: ETH \$1,821--\$3,357, TIA \$0.31--\$0.82), so another window would give different figures.
Reporting VWAP and percentiles reduces this effect but does not remove it.

\paragraph{Polkadot PoV estimate}
PoV sizes are not exposed on the relay chain, so we estimate them from 60 random blocks on each of 17 parachains (47\% of inclusions) as \texttt{System.BlockWeight} proof size plus extrinsic bytes, weighted by each parachain's share of inclusions. This uncompressed proxy overstates the real PoV. Two parachains report a fixed proof size that looks like a weight reservation; with them the mean is $\sim$1.13~MiB per candidate ($\sim$4.9~MiB/s), without them $\sim$0.29~MiB ($\sim$1.2~MiB/s). Tables report the higher figure; both are uncompressed proof weights that include state witnesses, so they are not comparable to rollup payload bytes.

\paragraph{Espresso Mainnet~0}
Mainnet~0 is a permissioned early deployment, so its cost, throughput and security figures cannot be compared on equal terms with permissionless, economically secured networks.
We include it for architectural completeness.

\paragraph{Single observation window}
A 92-day window is a snapshot, and behaviour under other market conditions, governance events or adoption levels may differ.
The released tooling can be used to extend the window.

\paragraph{Cost measures}
The six cost figures are of different kinds: Ethereum's is the blob base fee without the carrying transaction's execution gas; Celestia's quantiles use the gas price and exclude fixed PayForBlobs gas; NEAR's is a modelled marginal cost; Avail's is a per-transaction average; Polkadot's is a capacity price that also buys execution; Espresso's is a fixed placeholder. Spot fees also exclude batch overhead and compression, so they are not the effective cost to a rollup. The retention-adjusted cost (Table~\ref{tab:annualized_cost}, Fig.~\ref{fig:landscape}(b)) depends on the retention definition in Section~\ref{sec:meth-metrics}.

\paragraph{Data sources}
Celestia data comes from the Celenium indexer, Ethereum data from the public BigQuery dataset, and prices from CoinGecko hourly data; we did not cross-check these against independent sources beyond spot checks of protocol parameters at in-window heights.
